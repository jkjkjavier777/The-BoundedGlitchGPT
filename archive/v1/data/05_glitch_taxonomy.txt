BoundedGlitchEngine — A Taxonomy of Glitches

Not all glitches serve the same function, and treating them as
interchangeable is a common design mistake. Below is a working
taxonomy, developed through repeated observation of how irregularity
propagates through a bounded system.

Type One: The Drift Glitch. This is a slow, cumulative deviation from
baseline — nothing dramatic happens in any single cycle, but across
many cycles the system's output gradually shifts away from where it
started. Drift glitches are the hardest to catch because no individual
moment looks wrong. They are best detected by comparing long windows
of output rather than single samples.

Type Two: The Spike Glitch. Sudden, sharp, and short-lived. A spike
glitch appears for one or two cycles and then vanishes, often
triggered by an edge case in the input the Signal Layer wasn't tuned
to expect. Spike glitches are easy to detect but hard to predict,
since by definition they arise from conditions the system hasn't
modeled well.

Type Three: The Echo Glitch. This occurs when a deviation from an
earlier cycle re-enters the system through the feedback loop and
compounds with a new, unrelated deviation. Echo glitches are
dangerous specifically because they can look like coincidence — two
small irregularities that happen to align — when in fact they share
a common ancestor several cycles back.

Type Four: The Null Glitch. Paradoxically, this is the absence of
expected irregularity. A system that has been bounded too tightly for
too long stops glitching altogether, and that silence is itself a
signal that the Bounding Layer has drifted toward over-constraint.
An engine with zero glitches over a long window is not healthier than
one with occasional glitches — it is usually starved.

Type Five: The Resonant Glitch. The rarest and most consequential.
This happens when a glitch in one layer happens to match a structural
pattern already present in another layer, and the two reinforce each
other rather than cancel out. Resonant glitches can be productive —
sometimes they produce genuinely novel behavior worth keeping — or
destructive, if the resonance amplifies past what the Bounding Layer
can contain. Distinguishing the two in the moment is one of the open
problems in this framework.

Every glitch, regardless of type, should be logged with three
attributes: origin layer, propagation path, and containment outcome.
Without this record, the same glitch class tends to be rediscovered
repeatedly as if new, wasting effort that could go toward refining
the bounding logic instead.

The taxonomy is not closed. New glitch types are expected to emerge
as the engine is exposed to inputs it hasn't seen before — the
taxonomy itself should be treated as a living document, glitched and
bounded like everything else in the system.
